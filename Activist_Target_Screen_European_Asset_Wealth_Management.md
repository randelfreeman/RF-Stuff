# European Asset & Wealth Management — Activist Target Screen

## 20 structurally sound, mis-managed businesses with fixable problems

**Universe:** Listed asset managers, wealth managers, private-markets firms and investment platforms domiciled or primarily listed in the UK, Ireland and Continental Europe.
**Screen date:** August 2026
**Prepared for:** Internal idea generation — activist / constructivist engagement

---

## 1. Scope, method and screen criteria

### Sector and region

This screen covers **European (including UK) asset and wealth management** — a sector chosen because it exhibits, more consistently than almost any other, the exact combination the mandate calls for: structurally attractive unit economics (30–50% incremental margins, negative working capital, minimal capex), balance sheets that are net cash by regulatory design, registers that are almost entirely institutional, and share prices that have de-rated for five years against both their own history and global peers.

The sector is also, on the evidence, where activism is actually happening. In the first five months of 2026, 78 activist campaigns were launched in Europe, with the **UK accounting for nearly 42% of all European campaigns, up 30% year-on-year**, and the share of campaigns with an explicit M&A demand rising from 8% to 20%. One name on this list — Liontrust — is already the subject of a public open letter demanding a sale.

### Screen criteria applied

| Criterion | Threshold | Notes on application |
|---|---|---|
| Market capitalisation | > US$200m | Applied at 1 Aug 2026 FX |
| Balance sheet capacity | Net debt/EBITDA < 1.0x or net cash | See exceptions below |
| Ownership | No single holder > 40% | Applied strictly — this removed several otherwise-ideal targets |
| TSR | 3–5yr TSR below sector and index | Primary qualifying test |
| Business quality | Structurally sound franchise | Excludes genuinely broken or melting-ice-cube assets |
| Fixability | Identifiable, board-actionable remedies | Excludes names where the problem is the market, not the management |

### Screen exceptions — stated explicitly

Three names are included on merit despite failing a literal reading of the leverage test, because the underlying intent of that test (balance-sheet capacity to fund a capital return) is satisfied by a different metric:

- **Julius Baer (#15)** — a bank. Net debt/EBITDA is meaningless. Qualifies on **CET1 surplus** (~15% vs ~13.5% target, over CHF 1bn of distributable excess).
- **M&G (#14)** — an insurance-owned asset manager. Qualifies on **Solvency II surplus** (~220% coverage, ~£1bn shareholder surplus).
- **St. James's Place (#8)** — a life-company structure. Qualifies on **solvency surplus and cash-result generation**, not EBITDA leverage.

One name is included with a sector-fit caveat:

- **ABG Sundal Collier (#20)** — capital-markets-adjacent (advisory plus asset management) rather than a pure-play manager. Included because the activist thesis — compensation ratio, excess capital, sub-scale non-core division — is exactly the pattern this screen is designed to find.

### Data integrity note — read this before using any number

**The quantitative figures in this document are indicative and must be refreshed from Bloomberg, FactSet or CapitalIQ before any capital is committed or any approach is made.** They are assembled from public reporting, market data and sector research current to mid-2026, and are accurate enough to rank and prioritise but not to underwrite. Specifically:

- **TSR figures** are approximate total returns to 31 July 2026, and are compared against an indicative European asset & wealth management composite. They are directionally reliable; the precise basis points are not.
- **Shareholder registers** move constantly and disclosure thresholds differ by jurisdiction (3% UK, 3%/5% EU, 3% CH). Holdings shown are the most recent disclosed positions known and should be re-pulled from the regulatory filings and a share-register analysis before any campaign. **Percentages for index and quant holders in particular should be treated as approximations.**
- **Valuation multiples** are on a trailing or current-year consensus basis and are unadjusted for the very large net-cash positions several of these companies carry. Where cash is more than ~25% of market capitalisation, the **ex-cash** multiple is the one that matters, and it is materially lower than the headline figure shown.
- **Anything described as a company's likely defence is inference**, not a quotation.

## 1. Aberdeen Group plc (ABDN LN)

**Market cap ~£4.5bn · AUMA ~£520bn · UK**

| Metric | Value | vs sector |
|---|---|---|
| TSR 1yr | +38% | +19pp |
| TSR 3yr | +42% | +10pp |
| TSR 5yr | −22% | **−40pp** |
| EV/Sales | ~2.9x | In line |
| EV/EBIT | ~14x | Slight discount |
| P/E | ~14x | In line |
| FCF yield | ~7% | Attractive |
| EBIT margin | ~21% | **Below sector (28%)** |
| EBITDA margin | ~25% | Below sector |
| Net leverage | Net cash ~£0.5bn (after ~£0.6bn sub debt) | Passes |

**Top 5 shareholders (indicative):** BlackRock ~9%, Vanguard ~4%, Norges Bank ~3%, Legal & General ~3%, Dimensional ~3%. Register is essentially 100% institutional with no strategic or family holder — among the most open on this list.

### The activist pitch

- **Three unrelated businesses are stapled together and the market is refusing to pay for any of them.** Investments (a scale-challenged active manager), Adviser (a UK platform) and interactive investor (a fast-growing D2C platform) share almost no cost base, no clients and no distribution. On any sum-of-the-parts, **interactive investor alone approaches the value of the entire group** at platform peer multiples.
- **The cost programme is necessary but it is not a strategy.** £180m of savings delivered and a £150m+ target hit is competent execution — but it does not explain why these three businesses belong in one holding company, and it does not close a 5-year TSR gap of 40 points to a sector that is itself down against the index.
- **A 21% EBIT margin on £520bn of assets is structural under-earning**, roughly 7 points below sector. The gap is central overhead and a long tail of sub-scale investment strategies that will never earn their cost of capital.

### Most actionable changes

1. **Separate interactive investor** by demerger or IPO of a minority stake. It is the single largest, cleanest value-release event available anywhere on this list.
2. **Rationalise the investment platform** — close or sell strategies below a hard AUM/profitability floor, and set a public 28% EBIT margin target for FY28 with executive pay tied to it.
3. **Return £500m+ via buyback** from the surplus above regulatory requirement, and commit to a standing policy rather than ad-hoc announcements.

**Ease of building support: 4/5.** Fully institutional, no blocking stake, and holders have already demonstrated a willingness to force change at this company. The complication is that the current CEO's turnaround is working, which buys management time and makes a hostile posture harder to justify. The right approach is constructive and public-adjacent — a well-argued sum-of-the-parts letter, not a board fight.

**Expected company defence:** "The turnaround is delivering — profit up, costs down £180m, back in the FTSE 100, and 2026 targets of £300m+ adjusted operating profit reaffirmed. interactive investor is the growth engine and its cash funds group transformation; separating it now would forfeit exactly the value the market is beginning to recognise. Give us time to complete the plan."

**Activist angles:** Portfolio simplification (primary) · Capital allocation · Cost/margin discipline

## 2. Man Group plc (EMG LN)

**Market cap ~£1.8bn · AUM ~$180bn · UK**

| Metric | Value | vs sector |
|---|---|---|
| TSR 1yr | −18% | **−37pp** |
| TSR 3yr | −22% | **−54pp** |
| TSR 5yr | +5% | **−13pp** |
| EV/Sales | ~1.6x | **Deep discount** |
| EV/EBIT | ~7x | **Deep discount** |
| P/E | ~8x | **Deep discount** |
| FCF yield | ~11% | Very attractive |
| EBIT margin | ~22% | Below sector |
| EBITDA margin | ~25% | Below sector |
| Net leverage | Net cash ~$0.4bn plus ~$1bn+ seed investments | Passes comfortably |

**Top 5 shareholders (indicative):** BlackRock ~9%, Vanguard ~4%, Norges Bank ~3%, Schroders ~3%, Artemis ~3%. Fully institutional, no anchor.

### The activist pitch

- **The market pays nothing for the performance-fee option and nothing for the balance sheet.** At ~7x EBIT with over $1bn of seed capital and net cash on the balance sheet, the *ex-cash* multiple on the management-fee annuity alone is roughly 5x — a price that implies the franchise is in terminal decline. It is not: AUM is at or near record and flows have been positive.
- **The compensation ratio is the problem, and it is a board choice, not a market condition.** In a business where the balance sheet, the brand, the distribution and the technology belong to shareholders, employees are capturing a disproportionate share of a good year and shareholders are absorbing a full share of a bad one.
- **The multi-boutique sprawl has stopped generating scale.** AHL, GLG, Numeric, FRM and Varagon operate as near-independent units with duplicated infrastructure. There is a simplification dividend here that management has not attempted to collect.

### Most actionable changes

1. **Cap and publish the compensation ratio** with a hard ceiling, and tie a majority of executive LTIP vesting to EPS-per-share growth rather than AUM growth.
2. **Rotate the seed book into buybacks.** $1bn+ of balance-sheet seed capital earning a fund return is not a strategic asset when the stock trades at 7x EBIT. A programmatic $300–400m recycling into repurchases is immediately accretive.
3. **Consolidate the boutiques** onto a single operating platform and report a group-level cost:income target.

**Ease of building support: 4/5.** Clean register, deeply frustrated holders, an obvious and quantifiable ask. The difficulty is that Man's earnings are genuinely volatile, which gives management a permanent "we're at a cyclical trough" answer.

**Expected company defence:** "Quant performance mean-reverts and we have been here before; the franchise is intact and flows are positive. Seed capital is a competitive necessity — we cannot launch products without it, and it earns a return. Compensation is what retains the investment talent that generates the performance fees you are asking us to protect."

**Activist angles:** Capital allocation (primary) · Cost/compensation discipline · Portfolio simplification

**Top 5 shareholders (indicative):** Mark Coombs (founder-CEO) ~31%, BlackRock ~5%, Vanguard ~3%, Norges Bank ~3%, Aberdeen ~3%.

### The activist pitch

- **More than half the market capitalisation is cash and seed investments doing nothing strategic.** ~£750m of net cash and seed capital against a £1.4bn market cap means the operating business — a 40% EBIT margin franchise with $50bn of AUM — is being valued at roughly 5x EBIT. That is a price for a business in liquidation, and this one is now taking in money again ($1.3bn of net inflows in the June quarter, well ahead of consensus).
- **AUM has fallen from $98bn to $51bn and the cost base has not followed.** The firm is still resourced, seeded and organised for a business twice its current size. Every incremental AUM dollar recovered should be dropping through at 60%+ — it is not.
- **The founder holds 31%, which is not control.** The other 69% is a conventional institutional register that has watched a 63-point underperformance against a sector that is itself down. There is a coalition available here.

### Most actionable changes

1. **Return £300m+ through a tender offer.** A tender (rather than an open-market buyback) is the right instrument: it lets the founder participate or not, and at a 5x ex-cash EBIT multiple the accretion is extreme.
2. **Resize the cost base to a $50bn business**, with a public commitment to a 50% EBIT margin at current AUM and remuneration tied to it.
3. **Rotate seed capital out of proprietary EM funds.** Shareholders are taking concentrated emerging-market beta on the balance sheet that they did not ask for and are not being paid for.

**Ease of building support: 3/5.** Coombs' 31% is short of a veto on ordinary resolutions but it is a very loud voice, and he is the founder of a firm that still bears the imprint of his conviction about EM cyclicality. The path is to build a 15–20% coalition among the institutional 69% and make the tender offer an idea he can adopt as his own — he would be a large participant in the value created.

**Expected company defence:** "Emerging markets are cyclical, and the cycle is turning — flows have inflected positive and beat expectations. Capital is what allows us to seed products, support clients through drawdowns and survive periods no leveraged competitor could. We have run this business through three EM crises with the same balance-sheet philosophy and it is why we are still here."

**Activist angles:** Capital allocation (primary — excess cash) · Cost discipline · Governance (founder-CEO concentration)

### The activist pitch

- **Someone has already made it, publicly.** On 23 March 2026 the portfolio managers of the GAM Global Opportunities and GAM Global Special Situations funds published an open letter to CEO John Ions calling for an **immediate strategic review with the objective of selling the business to the highest bidder**, noting the share price had fallen approximately **85% from its September 2021 peak**. ### Most actionable changes

1. **Commence the strategic review.** The ask is already public and unanswered; the activist role is to convert one letter into a shareholder coalition that makes refusal untenable. A formal review with a stated deadline is the deliverable.
2. **Halt all further M&A pending the review**, and redirect free cash flow to buybacks — at an 18% FCF yield, retiring stock is the highest-return use of capital available to this board by a wide margin.
3. **Reconstitute the board.** A management team that has presided over an 85% decline should not be the team that runs the sale process; the review needs an independent chair and independent advisers with a clear mandate.

**Ease of building support: 5/5 — the easiest register on this list.** No controlling holder, no family, no strategic stake, a fully institutional shareholder base sitting on catastrophic losses, and a public activist letter already circulated to every one of them. This is a company where the hard work of persuading the register has largely been done by someone else.

**Expected company defence:** "Outflows are narrowing, AuMA is up to £24.3 billion, and River Global broadens our investment capability and distribution at a very modest price. A forced sale at a cyclical low in UK equity sentiment would crystallise the loss rather than recover it. The strategy needs time, not an auction."

**Screen note — the one name here that has *outperformed*.** Quilter is included deliberately as a **"phase two" target**: the recovery re-rating is largely complete, which means the easy money has been made and the remaining value is structural. ### The activist pitch

- **Three businesses that would each be worth more apart.** A platform (worth platform multiples), an advice network (worth distribution multiples) and an investment manager (worth asset-management multiples) are being valued as a single vertically-integrated conglomerate — which is to say, at the lowest of the three.
- **Vertical integration is now a regulatory liability, not a moat.** The FCA's scrutiny of ongoing advice services and vertically-integrated charging has changed the risk calculus. Quilter earns a margin at three points in the same value chain for the same client, which is precisely the structure the regulator is examining.
- **Cost:income near 72% against platform peers in the 50s.** Scale in this model was supposed to produce operating leverage. It has produced overhead.

### Most actionable changes

| EV/Sales | ~6.0x | Premium (justified by margin) |
| EV/EBIT | ~11x | **Deep discount to market-infrastructure peers (18–25x)** |
| P/E | ~14x | Discount |
| FCF yield | ~8% | Attractive |
| EBIT margin | ~40% | **Well above sector** |
| EBITDA margin | ~55–58% | **Highest on this list** |

**Top 5 shareholders (indicative):** Hellman & Friedman (via LHC vehicle) ~30% and reducing, BNP Paribas ~8%, UBS (inherited Credit Suisse position) ~4%, BlackRock ~3%, Capital Group ~3%.

### The activist pitch

- **This is market infrastructure being valued as a fund distributor.** €1.6 trillion of assets under administration and 55%+ EBITDA margins describe a network with genuine network effects — the closest thing European fund distribution has to a monopoly. Exchanges and market-infrastructure businesses with these characteristics trade at 18–25x EBIT. Allfunds trades at ~11x.
- **Euronext offered €8.75 per share in 2023 and walked away. The stock is now €6.44.** That bid — €5.5bn, a 19% premium at the time — established that a sophisticated strategic acquirer valued this asset at a substantial premium to where it trades three years later. The board has not since explained why shareholders should be content below that level.
- **The private-equity overhang has capped the stock for four years, and management has spent the period diversifying instead of monetising.** Hellman & Friedman's ~30% is a known, mechanical seller. Meanwhile capital and attention have gone into lower-return "data and solutions" adjacencies rather than defending and taxing the core network.

### Most actionable changes

1. **Run a formal strategic process.** The 2023 Euronext approach proved strategic appetite; the largest shareholder is an exit-seeking financial sponsor; and the public-market valuation is less than three-quarters of a bid the board already received. The alignment between the largest holder and an activist here is unusually strong.
2. **Refocus on the core network and divest or close the sub-scale data and technology-solutions lines.** These dilute the margin, complicate the equity story and compete for capital with the one asset that is genuinely defensible.
3. **Aggressive buyback while the overhang persists.** With net cash and an 8% FCF yield, the company should be the natural buyer of the stock H&F is selling — converting an overhang into per-share accretion instead of a discount.

**Ease of building support: 4/5.** The register's defining feature is that the ~30% holder *wants to sell*, which is the single most favourable condition for an M&A-oriented campaign. BNP at ~8% is a strategic holder with its own distribution interests. The remaining institutional base is deeply underwater from the IPO and would support almost any credible plan to close the gap to the Euronext price.

**Expected company defence:** "The network compounds — AUA is at record levels and the switching costs are enormous. The data and solutions businesses are the higher-margin future of the platform, not a distraction. Discussions about the ownership of the company are a matter for the board at the appropriate time, not for public negotiation."

**Activist angles:** M&A / strategic review (primary) · Portfolio simplification (exit non-core solutions) · Capital allocation (buyback against overhang)

**Top 5 shareholders (indicative):** Timone Fiduciaria (the managers' and partners' shareholder pact) ~24%, BlackRock ~5%, Norges Bank ~3%, Vanguard ~3%, Fidelity ~3%.

### The activist pitch

- **One of the cheapest asset managers in Europe at ~8x earnings, and the discount is a conglomerate discount the company built itself.** Italian retail distribution, a banking joint venture, Kairos, private markets, and a long tail of foreign subsidiaries across Turkey, Brazil, Australia, Mexico and beyond. No investor can model this. The market's response to complexity it cannot model is a permanent discount.
- **The banking project consumes capital, capital ratios and management attention for an uncertain return.** Building or acquiring a bank to secure distribution is a capital-intensive answer to a commercial problem, and it converts a capital-light 35%-margin asset manager into something the market must value on a bank multiple.
- **The Timone pact at ~24% controls the narrative without controlling the votes.** The partners' pact is a genuine alignment mechanism and a genuine governance obstacle at the same time — it delivers management continuity and it insulates the board from the other 76% of the register.

### Most actionable changes

1. **Kill or spin the banking project** and recommit to the capital-light distribution and asset-management model that produces the 35% margin.
2. **Divest the sub-scale foreign subsidiaries.** Turkey, Brazil, Australia and Mexico add complexity, currency exposure, governance load and negligible earnings. Exiting them is worth more in multiple than in proceeds.
3. **Simplify to a single reporting structure** with clean segment disclosure and a formal, published capital-return policy in place of the current discretionary approach.

**Ease of building support: 2/5 — the hardest campaign in Continental Europe on this list.** The Timone pact is cohesive, long-standing and philosophically committed to the current model; Italian AGM dynamics and the domestic retail register favour incumbents; and the partner network is also the distribution network, which means the people who would have to vote for change are the people the strategy is designed to enrich. A co-investment with a credible Italian institutional partner is close to mandatory, and even then this is a multi-year engagement.

**Expected company defence:** "The distribution network and the partner alignment *are* the business model — no other Italian manager has this. The bank secures our distribution channel permanently against the risk of being disintermediated. International operations are strategic optionality in growth markets. The dividend and the buybacks demonstrate our commitment to shareholders."

**Activist angles:** Portfolio simplification (primary — exit banking and sub-scale geographies) · Governance (pact structure, board independence) · Capital allocation

## 19. Deutsche Beteiligungs AG (DBAN GY)

**Market cap ~€0.48bn · AUM ~€2.5–3bn · Germany**

| Metric | Value | vs sector |
|---|---|---|
| TSR 1yr | 0% | −19pp |
| TSR 3yr | −20% | **−52pp** |
| TSR 5yr | −35% | **−53pp** |
| Discount to NAV | ~25–30% | Wide and persistent |
| EV/EBIT (fund services) | ~6x | **Deep discount** |
| P/E | n.m. (portfolio revaluations dominate) | — |
| FCF yield | n.m. | — |
| Fund-services EBIT margin | ~25% | Below sector |
| Net leverage | Net cash / minimal debt at AG level | Passes |

**Top 5 shareholders (indicative):** Rossmann Beteiligungs GmbH (Dirk Rossmann family) ~28%, Universal-Investment funds ~5%, Allianz Global Investors ~3%, Deutsche Balaton ~3%, plus a substantial and diffuse German retail base.

### The activist pitch

- **A sixty-year-old German Mittelstand private-equity franchise trading at a persistent 25–30% discount to a conservatively-marked NAV** that realisations have repeatedly validated. The discount is not a mark-to-market dispute; it is a structural and governance discount.
- **The balance sheet is used to co-invest rather than to buy back stock at seventy cents on the euro.** Every euro committed to a new co-investment at full price, while the company's own portfolio is available at a 30% discount, is a capital-allocation decision the board should have to defend.
- **Two economically distinct businesses that the market cannot value together.** A fund-management company earning fees (an annuity, worth a multiple of earnings) and a balance-sheet co-investment portfolio (worth NAV). Presented as one, the market applies a discount to both.

### Most actionable changes

1. **Separate — or at minimum separately report and guide — the fund-services business from the co-investment portfolio,** with a full fee-earning AUM and FRE disclosure so the annuity can be valued on its own terms.
2. **Institute a standing discount-control mechanism:** buy back stock whenever the discount to NAV exceeds 20%, funded by realisation proceeds ahead of new co-investment.
3. **Refresh the Supervisory Board** with independent private-markets professionals, and introduce full English-language reporting and an international investor-relations programme. A significant part of this discount is simply that global private-markets investors have never heard of the company.

**Ease of building support: 2/5.** Rossmann's ~28%, the German two-tier board structure (which insulates the management board from direct shareholder pressure) and a large, passive domestic retail register make this a long campaign. The mitigating factor is that the asks are conventional and hard to argue against publicly — discount control and segment disclosure are not radical demands, and German institutional holders and proxy advisers have become materially more receptive to both.

**Expected company defence:** "Our NAV is conservatively marked and our realisation history proves it — we have consistently exited above carrying value. Balance-sheet co-investment is what allows us to raise funds at all; LPs require it. We have focused on the German mid-market for six decades and that focus is the moat. The discount reflects small-cap illiquidity, not our strategy."

**Activist angles:** Capital allocation (primary — discount control) · Portfolio simplification (separate AM from balance sheet) · Governance and disclosure

### The activist pitch

- **The compensation ratio has captured essentially all of the value creation.** At a ~55–60% comp ratio, shareholders own a NOK 3bn stub with NOK 0.9bn of net cash sitting inside it. A 13–15% EBIT margin in a business with no capital intensity and no balance-sheet risk is not a market outcome; it is a distribution decision made by the people receiving the distribution.
- **The cost base does not flex down in weak markets.** The comp ratio expands in downturns and does not fully contract in upturns — a ratchet that transfers cyclical risk to shareholders and cyclical reward to employees.
- **The asset-management business is a hidden, sub-scale asset.** ABG's fund-management and real-estate fund operations are worth materially more inside a Nordic consolidator than as an appendage to an advisory business, and their earnings are invisible inside group reporting.

### Most actionable changes

1. **Cap the compensation ratio at 50% with a published, board-approved policy**, and tie non-executive and management board remuneration to adherence. Every point of comp ratio is roughly a point of EBIT margin.
2. **Separate or sell the asset-management business** to a Nordic consolidator, and disclose its standalone economics in the interim.
3. **Return the excess NOK 900m+ of balance-sheet cash via tender offer.** The advisory business needs a fraction of this capital; the rest is a permanent drag on return on equity.

**Ease of building support: 3/5.** No controlling holder and a straightforward Norwegian governance framework where shareholders can requisition and vote effectively. The obstacle is the ~25% employee and partner bloc, which is the direct beneficiary of the compensation status quo and will vote as one on any comp-ratio resolution. Norwegian institutions are, however, unusually receptive to governance and remuneration arguments, and the arithmetic here is very easy to explain.

**Expected company defence:** "The compensation ratio is the price of retaining senior bankers in a small, intensely competitive Nordic market — cap it and the revenue leaves with the people. Balance-sheet capital supports underwriting commitments and market-making, and regulatory capital requirements are not optional. Advisory is cyclical and we are near a trough; the dividend has been maintained throughout."

**Activist angles:** Cost / compensation discipline (primary) · Portfolio simplification (separate asset management) · Capital allocation (excess cash)

# 4. Cross-cutting observations

### The four repeating patterns

Across all twenty names, essentially the same four problems recur — which is itself the strongest argument that these are management and board failures rather than market conditions:

**1. Idle balance sheets (12 of 20 names).** Ashmore, Impax, IntegraFin, Man Group, Ninety One, ABG Sundal Collier and others hold net cash equal to 10–50% of market capitalisation while their own equity trades at 5–8x EBIT. In several cases the arithmetic is stark enough that a single tender offer would be the highest-return capital deployment available to the board by an order of magnitude. Boards defend this cash as "regulatory," "client trust" or "seed capital" — but almost none of them publish the calculation that separates required capital from surplus. **Forcing that disclosure is the single most repeatable ask across the whole list.**

**2. Cost bases sized for assets that left (9 of 20).** Ashmore, Impax, Jupiter, Ninety One, Liontrust and Julius Baer all run cost structures built for a larger business. The tell is consistent: margins that have fallen 8–15 points while revenue fell, meaning almost no cost flexed with the decline. Jupiter is the useful precedent for every other name here — it took cost:income from 82% to 77% inside a year through the CCLA integration, proving the cost was always removable.

**3. Structural complexity that no investor can model (8 of 20).** Aberdeen's three unrelated businesses, Ninety One's dual-listed structure, Quilter's vertical integration, M&G's insurance-plus-asset-management hybrid, Eurazeo's and Deutsche Beteiligungs' balance-sheet-plus-fee-stream mix, Azimut's bank and geographic sprawl. In every case the market resolves the ambiguity in the same direction: it applies the lowest defensible multiple to the whole. Simplification is the highest-value single action available at Aberdeen, Ninety One, M&G, Eurazeo and Allfunds.

**4. Boards that have presided through the entire de-rating (all 20).** Founder-chairs at Foresight, founder-CEOs at Ashmore and Impax, family blocs at Eurazeo and Deutsche Beteiligungs, partner pacts at Azimut, and serially-reconstituted boards at Julius Baer and St. James's Place. Governance is the wedge issue in nearly every campaign here — not because governance itself creates value, but because governance resolutions are the ones proxy advisers and index funds support almost mechanically, which makes them the cheapest way to establish that a board can in fact be moved.

### On sequencing

The right first ask at most of these companies is a **disclosure** ask, not a structural one. Required-versus-surplus capital, fee-earning AUM and FRE, platform unit economics, segment-level margins — these are impossible to refuse in public, cost the company nothing, and in roughly half these cases the disclosure itself does substantial work on the discount. It also establishes the engagement and reveals which boards will move without a fight.

### On the Continental European problem

**The strict "no >40% holder" test removes most of Continental Europe.** Amundi, DWS, Banca Generali, Banca Mediolanum, Vontobel, EFG, Tikehau, Antin and DeA Capital are all excluded on ownership alone, and several of them would otherwise rank among the very best targets on economics. This is the structural reason UK activism accounts for ~42% of European campaigns despite the UK being a much smaller share of European market capitalisation: **the UK is where the registers are actually open.** Any European mandate in this sector will be UK-weighted whether or not that is the intention, and the six Continental names here are correspondingly harder campaigns — four of the six sit in the bottom tier on support ease.
