Stance: Underweight / Avoid on fundamentals; trade the event, do not own the NAV. Hanjin KAL is not a discounted holding company. It trades at roughly 2.8x the value of what it owns because two blocs (Chairman Cho Won-tae's camp and Hoban Group) are 0.42 percentage points apart on the register and the market is pricing an open-ended control auction. Our probability-weighted 12-month value is about KRW 105,000 (roughly 27% below the reference price), with a bull case of KRW 170,000 on a tender offer and a bear case of KRW 65,000 if the contest is settled without a bid. The cheaper expression of every fundamental thesis inside this group is Korean Air itself (003490 KS), which trades near 1.0x the value the market implicitly assigns to it through Hanjin KAL.

1. Executive summary

  • What it owns. Two listed stakes, Korean Air 26.13% (about 96.2m shares, worth about KRW 2.87tn) and Hanjin Transportation 30.78% (about 4.6m shares, worth about KRW 68bn), plus a small unlisted portfolio (Jungseok Enterprise real estate 60.49%, KAL Hotel Network 100%, Topas Travel Information 94.35%, Hanjin Tourism 100%) that we value at roughly KRW 0.4 to 0.8tn. Holdco net debt is small after the January 2026 hotel disposal (we estimate KRW 50 to 100bn at end-March 2026, versus KRW 878bn at end-2021).
  • What it is worth. Base-case NAV of about KRW 3.4tn, or KRW 51,000 per share. IM Securities' published NAV is KRW 2.65tn. The stock capitalises the company at KRW 9.58tn. Every KRW 1 of look-through Korean Air is being bought for about KRW 2.8.
  • Why it trades there. Hoban Group has spent KRW 878bn since 2022 to accumulate 20.15% (average cost about KRW 65,300) and sits 0.42pp behind the Cho family bloc at 20.57%. Korea Development Bank's (KDB) 10.58% block, bought in 2020 to fund the Asiana rescue, is slated for sale after the integrated Korean Air launches on 17 December 2026. Japan Airlines bought an undisclosed stake below 5% on 4 September 2026 as a declared partner of Korean Air. The stock rose about 21% in the month to early September.
  • Why the premium is fragile. Voting arithmetic favours the incumbent: Cho bloc 20.57% plus Delta 14.90%, KDB 10.58%, the employee welfare fund 0.66%, JAL (under 5%) and the Jungseok Inha Academy's in-progress purchase (+1.16pp) sum to roughly 48 to 50%. Hoban cannot win a proxy contest without KDB's block, and KDB has signalled a transparent competitive block sale, which Cho's allies can contest. A police raid on 7 September 2026 over the 2025 treasury-share contribution to the employee fund adds legal, not economic, risk.
  • What changed on 10 September 2026. The second Commercial Act amendment took effect: cumulative voting is now mandatory for listed companies with assets of KRW 2tn or more and two audit committee members must be elected separately under the 3% voting cap. Hoban can now win board representation with 20% without buying another share. This is the strongest argument that the "buy-more" phase of the contest is ending, which removes the marginal bid that supports the premium.
  • Management's record on the discount is mixed: real progress on de-leveraging (Jin Air sale, Waikiki hotel, Seosomun building, Grand Hyatt West Tower) and a formal 50% payout on adjusted separate earnings, but no buybacks, a rights issue to KDB in 2020, and a series of control-motivated uses of capital (treasury shares gifted to the employee fund, repurchase of Jungseok Enterprise shares, purchases of Hanjin shares, foundation buying of Hanjin KAL stock).
  • Activist angle. There is no conventional "close the discount" campaign to run at 2.8x NAV. The live activist plays are governance and M&A: board seats via cumulative voting and the separate audit-committee election (Hoban's four entities carry about 6.3% effective votes under the 3% cap versus 3% for the Cho bloc), scrutiny of related-party transactions, and pressure for a full pass-through of subsidiary dividends once the current dividend policy expires with FY2026. For a fund, the value-realisation trade is long Korean Air, not long Hanjin KAL.

2. Bull case: why the premium could hold or widen

  1. A live two-bidder situation with deep pockets on one side. Hoban sits on about KRW 1.05tn of unrealised gains and has stated ambitions in aviation (it bid for Kumho Industrial, Asiana's parent, in 2015). Buying up to 24.9% would keep it below the tender-offer trigger while making it the largest single holder. The July 2026 purchase of 1.13m shares (at least KRW 120bn, over KRW 106,000 per share) shows willingness to pay well above NAV.
  2. KDB's block is the fulcrum, and it will be sold. KDB's 10.58% (about 7.06m shares, about KRW 1.0tn at market) is expected to be sold via a competitive block process from 2027. A competitive auction for a control-deciding block is the single most powerful catalyst for a premium to widen, and market participants have already flagged Hoban as the natural highest bidder.
  3. The incumbent must respond with cash it does not have. Chairman Cho holds 5.78% personally, much of it pledged (KDB holds a pledge over the full 5.78%; bank and broker loans funded inheritance tax instalments). The defence is being outsourced to a school foundation (Jungseok Inha Academy is buying 773,676 shares for KRW 102bn) and to airline partners (Delta, JAL). Each of these buyers is price-insensitive, which supports the tape.
  4. Tender offer optionality. Under Korean rules, a public tender offer is the only fast route to a decisive stake. Hoban at 20.15% plus a 15 to 20% tender at a 20 to 30% premium is financeable for a group with its cash position. The Korea Zinc precedent (2024-25) showed that Korean control contests can escalate to serial tender offers at multiples of intrinsic value.
  5. Underlying NAV can grow. Korean Air's integrated airline launches 17 December 2026; brokers (Mirae, Shinhan, KB) raised targets to KRW 36,000 to 37,000 in September 2026 on lower jet fuel and firm cargo yields. Every KRW 1,000 on Korean Air adds about KRW 96bn (KRW 1,440 per share) to Hanjin KAL's NAV.
  6. Balance sheet is clean and dividend capacity is rising. Holdco net debt has fallen from KRW 878bn (2021) to a low double-digit billion range after recovering KRW 230bn of loans from KAL Hotel Network in Q1 2026. Dividend income was KRW 88bn in 2025 and brand royalties about KRW 40bn. The 50% payout policy expires with FY2026 and must be re-set, giving management an easy lever to announce a higher payout during a control contest.
  7. Governance reform is a tailwind for a minority-friendly settlement. The 2025-26 Commercial Act amendments (directors' duty to shareholders, mandatory treasury share cancellation, mandatory cumulative voting) raise the cost of the historical defensive playbook and increase the chance that any resolution involves value being paid to all shareholders rather than to insiders.

3. Bear case: why the premium collapses or stays static without a bid

  1. The vote is not close; the register only looks close. Cho bloc 20.57% plus Delta 14.90% plus KDB 10.58% plus welfare fund 0.66% plus JAL (under 5%) plus Jungseok Inha's incremental 1.16% gives the incumbent roughly 48 to 50% of the common. Hoban (20.15%) plus NPS (5.11%) plus the entire remaining float (about 25%) could not out-vote that even with perfect turnout. Both airline shareholders have publicly stated support for management.
  2. KDB is unlikely to hand the block to a hostile bidder. KDB's 2020 investment agreement gives it three board nominees and consent rights; its stated rationale for the exit is orderly completion of the airline consolidation. A transparent block sale is likely to be structured (size, timing, syndication to Delta/JAL/foundations) to avoid handing control to a construction group with no aviation licence experience. The market is pricing a coin-flip; the base rate for a state lender enabling a hostile change of control in a flag carrier is far lower.
  3. Cumulative voting removes the reason to keep buying. From today, Hoban can secure one board seat at the March 2027 AGM with its existing 20% (three outside directors' terms expire, including one separately elected audit committee seat under the 3% cap). Once board access is available at zero marginal cost, the marginal buyer disappears and the "premium for the next tranche" evaporates.
  4. The premium is the largest of any Korean holdco and is supported by nothing else. P/NAV 2.8x, P/B of roughly 3x, a 0.25% dividend yield and look-through P/E of about 37x versus a Korean holdco peer set that trades at 40 to 65% discounts to NAV. IM Securities called the July valuation "+240% versus NAV" and said the contest was already priced.
  5. Fundamental NAV is under pressure in 2026. Korean Air's Q2 2026 net loss of KRW 97bn (jet fuel above USD 210/bbl in March to April during the US-Iran conflict, FX losses of KRW 145bn) flowed through as an KRW 86bn consolidated net loss at Hanjin KAL in Q2. Consolidated net debt at Korean Air reached KRW 17.7tn at March 2026 with Asiana consolidated, and the merger will issue 20.34m new Korean Air shares, cutting Hanjin KAL's stake to 24.76%.
  6. Legal overhang is now real. The Seoul Metropolitan Police financial crimes unit raided Hanjin KAL on 7 September 2026 over the May 2025 contribution of 440,044 treasury shares (about KRW 66bn) to the employee welfare fund, alleging breach of trust (the fund became a special related party and its voting rights lifted Cho's reported stake). An indictment would not change control but would harden NPS and foreign votes against management and could invalidate the fund's alignment.
  7. Capital allocation is control-first, shareholder-second. Recent uses of cash: KRW 52bn to buy back Jungseok Enterprise shares from Korea Zinc, KRW 18bn for Hanjin shares, KRW 200bn lent to the loss-making hotel arm (since recovered), executive pay cap raised by KRW 12bn while the dividend stayed at KRW 360. None of this narrows the gap between price and value; it widens the governance discount that would apply once the control premium fades.
  8. Exit liquidity is thin. Free float is about 25 to 27% and shrinking (foundation and JAL purchases). Any de-escalation (Hoban reclassifying its holding, a settlement with a board seat, an agreed KDB placement) would see a crowded event-driven register exit into a market with no fundamental bid until roughly KRW 60,000 to 70,000.

6. Treasury shares

  • Hanjin KAL held 440,044 common treasury shares (0.66%) until 15 May 2025, when the board contributed them to the in-house employee welfare fund. The transfer converted non-voting shares into votes exercisable by a fund whose trustees are appointed by management; it was booked as a KRW 52.7bn expense.
  • Residual holdings of 4 common shares and 6,469 preferred shares were cancelled on 25 August 2026 (board decision 13 August; book value KRW 54m).
  • Current treasury position: effectively zero. Under the third Commercial Act amendment (effective 6 March 2026), any future repurchase must be cancelled within one year, so buybacks can no longer be used to build a defensive block. This is relevant to the activist case: a buyback would now be pure capital return, which management has never done.
  • Legal note: a citizens' group filed a criminal complaint in 2025 alleging that the contribution had "no recognisable necessity other than defending control"; the Seoul Metropolitan Police executed search warrants on 7 September 2026. Hanjin KAL says the contribution was for employee welfare.

Is the valuation too high? On every fundamental measure, yes. The stock is a call option on a control transaction, with about KRW 6.2tn of the KRW 9.6tn market cap (roughly 65%) representing option value over base NAV. For that option to pay out, someone must be willing to pay more than KRW 143,500 for the marginal share, which means paying about 2.8x for a 26% (soon 24.8%) minority stake in an airline that is itself fairly valued. Only a bidder who values control of Korean Air's KRW 11tn equity at a price close to KRW 36tn would do so rationally. Hoban's incremental purchases at KRW 106,000 or more show that "rational" is not the constraint in the short run; the constraint is whether the contest can actually be won, and the register says it cannot without KDB.

11. Pre-mortem: it is September 2028 and the stock is at KRW 60,000. What happened?

  1. KDB sold to the incumbent's syndicate. The 2027 block sale ran as a competitive process, but the block was split and placed with Delta, JAL, a domestic financial investor and the group foundations at a price only slightly above market. Hoban bid but was not awarded the whole block; the "coin-flip" became a certainty and the premium had nothing left to price.
  2. Cumulative voting satisfied Hoban. Hoban took one outside-director seat at the March 2027 AGM using its 20% and then reclassified its holding as "management participation" only to protect that seat. Board access reduced its need to own more stock; it began to sell down after the integrated airline's first results.
  3. The treasury-share case ended in an indictment, then a settlement. The court did not unwind the contribution, but the fund's votes were frozen pending appeal. The reputational damage cost management the NPS vote and prompted a governance settlement (an independent chair, an audit committee majority not aligned with either bloc) that made a hostile bid pointless.
  4. NAV fell while the premium collapsed. The integrated Korean Air carried KRW 18tn of net debt into a softer cargo cycle; jet fuel stayed volatile; 2027 earnings disappointed against the "merger synergy" narrative, and Korean Air de-rated to KRW 24,000. Hanjin KAL's NAV per share fell to about KRW 45,000; at 1.3x NAV the stock was KRW 60,000.
  5. The family's liquidity needs surfaced. Cho Seung-yeon's Jungseok stake was bought by the holdco; the foundation purchases were challenged by the education ministry; margin calls on the chairman's pledged shares forced a small sale. None of it changed control; all of it reminded investors who the capital was being allocated for.
  6. Index and flow effects. As the float shrank below 25% and turnover fell, index weights were cut. Event-driven funds that had crowded into the name on the JAL headline sold into an air pocket.

Early-warning indicators for this path: Hoban's 5% filing stays "simple investment" past the January 2027 AGM notice deadline; KDB announces a syndicated rather than single-buyer process; Delta/JAL increase stakes; Hanjin KAL board shrinks or staggers terms ahead of the AGM; a court or prosecution decision on the welfare-fund shares.

12. Contrarian view: what the market is refusing to see

  1. The contest has already been decided by the register, not by the tape. Consensus treats 20.57% versus 20.15% as a dead heat. It is not; the relevant comparison is about 49% versus 20% once allies are counted, and Hoban knows it (it voted for Cho in March 2026). The price is discounting a fight that the challenger has shown no sign of starting.
  2. Governance reform is bearish for the premium, not bullish. The consensus narrative is that the Commercial Act amendments help challengers. They do, but at the level of board seats, not control. When a 20% holder can obtain representation for free (mandatory cumulative voting from 10 September 2026, separate audit-committee election under the 3% cap where Hoban's four entities carry more effective votes than the entire Cho bloc), the economic rationale for paying 2.8x NAV for additional shares disappears. Reform converts a stock-accumulation contest into a boardroom contest.
  3. The person with the strongest incentive to transact is the chairman, not Hoban. Cho Won-tae's personal position is illiquid (5.78%, pledged, inheritance-tax loans, interest costs consuming pay and dividends) and the defence depends on other people's balance sheets. A negotiated outcome that monetises part of the family's position at a premium to friendly parties (airline partners or a financial investor) is more plausible than a hostile takeover, and such an outcome would be executed off-market, leaving minorities with a stock that returns to NAV.
  4. The cheap way to own every "bull" argument is Korean Air. Merger synergies, fuel relief, cargo strength, the value-up 30% payout and JAL/Delta partnerships all accrue to Korean Air at about 11x earnings and 1.0x book. Hanjin KAL offers the same exposure at 2.8x. A long Korean Air / short Hanjin KAL pair captures the premium compression with limited fundamental risk; the residual risk is a tender offer, which can be sized against (Hoban would need roughly KRW 1.5 to 2.5tn of cash at current prices for a decisive tender).
  5. The preferred shares are the sleeper. The non-voting preferred (18064K) trades at a 75 to 80% discount to the common and fell about 34% in the year to May 2026 while the common rallied, because it has no control value. Anyone who believes the NAV thesis but not the control thesis has a cheaper instrument; anyone who believes the control thesis has a clean hedge (long common / short preferred is the crowded trade; the unwind runs the other way).
  6. What could make the contrarian wrong. A decision by KDB to sell the entire 10.58% to a single highest bidder (which would be Hoban), or a Hoban tender offer before the March 2027 AGM. Both would be visible in filings weeks ahead; neither is consistent with Hoban's behaviour to date.

13. Shareholder activist case

13.1 Is there a classic activist case?

Not on valuation. Activism in holding companies usually attacks a discount: demand buybacks, special dividends, asset sales or a holdco-opco merger to force price toward NAV. At 2.8x NAV every one of those levers destroys value for Hanjin KAL holders (a merger with Korean Air, for example, would exchange KRW 143,500 of stock for about KRW 51,000 of Korean Air). The only classic lever that helps is a higher pass-through of subsidiary cash, and that adds at most KRW 1,000 of DPS.

13.3 Verdict on activism

  • The setting is unusually favourable for a governance-style campaign (a 20% challenger, a state holder that has already voted against the chairman via NPS, new cumulative-voting and 3%-rule mechanics, a live criminal investigation and a chairman with limited personal liquidity). Board seats for the challenger at the March 2027 AGM are the base case.
  • The setting is unfavourable for a value-realisation campaign because the stock already trades far above realisable value. An activist fund entering at KRW 143,500 would be paying KRW 6tn for the right to argue about KRW 0.6tn of asset sales.
  • The activist trade with positive expected value inside this group is in Korean Air: pressure for a higher payout (the 30% cap is on separate net income, which the merger and fuel costs depress), balance-sheet discipline after the Asiana consolidation, and clarity on the brand-royalty and related-party flows to the holdco.

15. Bottom line

Hanjin KAL is a control-contest instrument whose price has detached from the KRW 3.4tn of assets behind it. The contest is real, the register is not as close as the headlines suggest, and the newest governance rules make it cheaper for the challenger to win influence without buying shares. The premium can persist while foundations, airline partners and Hoban keep buying, and it can spike on a tender offer, but its expected path is down. We would not own the common for NAV, would express any positive view on the group through Korean Air, and would treat Hanjin KAL as a short candidate sized for a fat right tail, or as a paired trade against Korean Air, with a hard stop on a change in Hoban's filed purpose or on any single-buyer structure for the KDB block.
