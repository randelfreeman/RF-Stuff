## 1. Executive summary and investment view

**Recommendation: HOLD / Speculative Special-Situation. Not a yield instrument.**

Hui Xian REIT is one of the most extreme dislocations in Asian listed real estate — and, on our analysis, one of the most *justified* ones. The Trust owns a genuinely irreplaceable asset (Beijing Oriental Plaza, the c.800,000 sqm mixed-use complex at the junction of Wangfujing and Chang'an Avenue, one block from Tiananmen Square) yet trades at roughly **RMB0.50 per unit against an estimated book NAV of c.RMB3.2–3.4 per unit (E)** — a discount of c.85% — while yielding **under 1%**.

The paradox resolves once you follow the cash:

- **The properties still work.** FY2025 revenue of **RMB2,209m (R)** and net property income of an estimated **c.RMB1,130m (E)** imply an NPI margin above 50% and an implied capitalisation rate on enterprise value of roughly **16–17% (E)**. On asset economics alone the units look absurdly cheap.
- **The cash does not reach unitholders.** FY2025 Total Amount Available for Distribution was **RMB28m (R)** — roughly **2.5% of NPI**. DPU was **RMB0.0043 (R)**, versus RMB0.0935 in 2021 **(R)**. Distributions have fallen c.95% in four years.
- **The gap is structural, not cyclical.** Three forces compound: (i) a **currency mismatch** — 66% of debt was HKD-denominated at end-2024 **(R)** against 100% RMB assets, with **no hedging in place at the balance-sheet date (R)**; (ii) **aggressive deleveraging** funded from operating cash flow rather than distributions; and (iii) **PRC tax and JV cash-extraction leakage** between property-level NPI and offshore distributable cash.
- **Every debt repayment has crystallised a loss.** Chinese financial media, citing the Trust's own announcements, count **five debt repayments over two years, all loss-making**: HK$1.8bn repaid November 2024 crystallised c.**RMB209m (R)** of realised FX loss; HK$800m repaid and announced April 2026 crystallised c.**RMB44m (R)** — equal to **157% of the entire FY2025 distributable amount (R)**. FY2024 realised exchange losses totalled c.**RMB368m (R)**, single-handedly cutting distributable income from RMB227m to RMB26m **(R)**.
- **Operations are still deteriorating.** 1H2026 revenue fell **5.1% to RMB1,049m (R)** with a net loss of **RMB469m (R)**, wider than 1H2025's RMB404m **(R)**. Beijing Grade A office rents fell **16.3% YoY** to RMB210/sqm/month with vacancy at 15.2% **(R)**; FY2025 office NPI fell RMB59m to **RMB685m (R)** and retail NPI fell RMB110m to **RMB274m (R)**.

**What the market may be missing (contrarian view).** The two largest drags are *reversing simultaneously and mechanically*. One-month HIBOR has fallen from 5.27% (end-2023) to **2.63% (19 August 2026) (R)**, which on a c.RMB5bn book is worth roughly **RMB130m a year** of interest saving. And the RMB has *appreciated* — USD/CNY at **6.72 (21 August 2026), +3.4% YTD (R)** — which turns the HKD-debt translation from a headwind into a tailwind. If FX losses stop and interest normalises, distributable income has the arithmetic capacity to recover to **RMB250–400m (E)**, implying a DPU of RMB0.04–0.06 and, at a peer yield of 8–10%, a unit price of **RMB0.45–0.70**.

**Why we still say HOLD, not BUY.** The Trust has repeatedly demonstrated that it will use recovering cash flow to repay debt rather than distribute it, and it has repeatedly chosen *not* to hedge a mismatch it has understood since 2011. Until the Manager either hedges the currency book or commits to a distribution policy, the recovery accrues to lenders, not unitholders. Add a **wasting leasehold** — the Beijing Oriental Plaza commercial land use right runs to **6 December 2049 (R)**, i.e. c.23 years — and a book NAV that is not a terminal value but a melting one.

**Price target framework (Section 23):** Bear **RMB0.22**, Base **RMB0.52**, Bull **RMB0.95**. The distribution of outcomes is unusually wide and bimodal; position sizing matters more than the point estimate.

## 2. A layman's guide: what this company actually is

**In one sentence:** Hui Xian REIT is a Hong Kong-listed landlord that owns one enormous, famous building complex in central Beijing plus three smaller Chinese properties, collects rent and hotel revenue in yuan, and passes what is left over to investors — except that almost nothing has been left over for the last three years.

### How it is put together

Think of it as a chain of boxes:

1. **You** buy a *unit* on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange, priced and traded in offshore RMB.
2. Those units are in **Hui Xian REIT**, a Hong Kong trust regulated by the Securities and Futures Commission under the REIT Code.
3. The Trust does not employ anyone. It is run by an external **Manager**, Hui Xian Asset Management Limited, which is **70%-owned by CK Asset Holdings** (the Li Ka-shing group property company) and **30% by ARA Asset Management** (now part of ESR Group) **(R)**. The Manager charges fees, part of which it takes in new units rather than cash.
4. The Trust holds **Hui Xian (B.V.I.) Limited**, which holds **Hui Xian Investment Limited**, which is the foreign partner in a Sino-foreign co-operative joint venture called **Beijing Oriental Plaza Co., Ltd. ("BOP")**. Hui Xian Investment holds c.**99.99999983% of BOP and is entitled to 100% of its distributions (R)**.
5. **BOP owns the land use right and building ownership rights to Beijing Oriental Plaza** — but only until the land use right expires. For the commercial land, that is **6 December 2049 (R)**.

That structure matters enormously and is explained in Section 8. In plain terms: *the money is made inside China, taxed inside China, and has to be walked out of China through a joint venture before it can be paid to a Hong Kong unitholder in yuan.*

### The five ways it makes money

| Division (reporting segment) | What it is | How it earns | Rough share of revenue |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The Tower Offices** | 309,552 sqm of offices in Beijing Oriental Plaza **(R)** | Multi-year leases to corporate tenants; rent per sqm per month plus management fees | Largest single contributor; c.30% of revenue, c.60% of NPI **(E)** |
| **The Malls** | c.130,195 sqm three-level shopping mall at Beijing Oriental Plaza, plus car parks **(R)** | Base rent plus turnover rent from retailers; car park income | c.20% of revenue, c.20% of NPI **(E)** |
| **The Tower Apartments** | Serviced apartment towers at Beijing Oriental Plaza | Nightly/monthly serviced-apartment rates — hotel-like, not lease-like | c.8% of revenue **(E)** |
| **Hotels** | Grand Hyatt Beijing (in BOP), The Westin Shenyang (formerly Sofitel Shenyang Lido), Sheraton Chengdu Lido, Hyatt Regency Chongqing **(R)** | Room revenue, F&B, banqueting — an *operating business*, not a lease | c.30% of revenue but only c.10% of NPI **(E)** |
| **Chongqing Property** | Metropolitan Oriental Plaza, Jiefangbei CBD: c.90,000 sqm mall, over 54,000 sqm Grade A office, 353 car parks **(R)** | Retail and office leases | c.12% of revenue **(E)** |

The critical point a newcomer must grasp: **roughly 30% of revenue comes from hotels, which are high-revenue, low-margin operating businesses.** That is why revenue of RMB2.2bn converts to NPI of only c.RMB1.1bn. A pure office/retail REIT of the same revenue would produce far more NPI. Hotels also carry real depreciation and refurbishment capex that a leased office does not.

### Why the distribution collapsed — the simple version

Imagine a landlord who collects RMB1.1bn of net rent a year. Out of that, they must pay:

- interest on a c.RMB5.8bn mortgage, *two-thirds of which is denominated in Hong Kong dollars*;
- Chinese corporate income tax, then a withholding tax to get the money out of China;
- fees to the external Manager;
- refurbishment capex on four hotels and a 25-year-old mall.

Then, on top of all that, the landlord chose to **repay large chunks of the mortgage early** — and because the mortgage is in HK dollars while the rent is in yuan, and the yuan had weakened, **each repayment crystallised a currency loss**. HK$1.8bn repaid in November 2024 cost c.RMB209m in realised FX loss **(R)**. Nothing was hedged **(R)**.

Result: RMB1.1bn of net rent became **RMB28m** of distributable cash **(R)**. That is the entire investment case in one paragraph.

Hui Xian REIT is a **single-asset-dominant, mixed-use, RMB-denominated leasehold REIT with an embedded hotel operating business and an unhedged HKD funding structure.** Each of those five clauses is a driver.

## 4. Macro and micro factors: tailwinds and headwinds

### 4.1 Macro

**Chinese office cycle.** Beijing Grade A office is in the deepest downcycle of the modern era. Average rents fell **5.6% QoQ and 16.3% YoY to RMB210/sqm/month**, with the overall vacancy rate at **15.2%** at end-December 2025 and forecast rents down a further **6.6% in 2026 (R)**. There is a genuine glimmer: **no new supply for twelve months has driven six consecutive quarters of falling vacancy to Q2 2026 (R)**, and the *rate* of rent decline is decelerating. Landlords in CBD and Wangjing have absorbed space using rent concessions **(R)** — meaning headline rent understates the true economic decline once free-rent periods are amortised.

**Chinese consumption and the retail bifurcation.** Chinese retail is not weak; it is *bifurcated*. Beijing Taikoo Li Sanlitun grew sales **63.2% in 1H2026 with rental income up 16% (R)**, concentrating Hermès, Dior, Louis Vuitton, Tiffany and Loro Piana. Trophy assets are compounding; everything else is compressing. Hui Xian's Beijing mall sits in the difficult middle — a famous address with a mass-premium tenant mix — and its FY2025 retail NPI fell **RMB110m (R)**.

**Hotels.** Nationally, China hotel RevPAR fell **6% in July 2026**, with occupancy −3pp and ADR −1% **(R)**, as domestic tourism price competition intensified. Beijing specifically saw 2025 occupancy fall **2.1pp to 64.5%** and ADR fall **4.2% to RMB585 (R)**. The offsetting signal: the *luxury* tier is outperforming — Hyatt reported Greater China RevPAR **+12.4% in Q1 2026 and +7.2% in Q2 2026**, citing leisure luxury **(R)**, and inbound travel to Beijing is recovering. Hui Xian's hotels are all in the upper-upscale/luxury tier, so it should capture more of the good news than the bad.

**Rates.** This is the single most important macro variable for *distributable* income and it has moved decisively in the Trust's favour. One-month HIBOR: **2.71% (Jan-2023) to 5.27% (Dec-2023) to c.3.18% (Dec-2025) to 2.63% (19 Aug 2026) (R)**, with an extraordinary collapse to near zero in May–June 2025 when HKMA intervention flooded the system with liquidity **(R)**. Consensus expects 1M HIBOR to settle around **2.26% by end-2026** with a 2–3% range thereafter **(R)**.

**Currency.** USD/CNY at **6.7219 on 21 August 2026, with CNY +3.42% against USD year-to-date (R)**. Because the HKD is pegged to the USD, an appreciating RMB *reduces* the RMB value of HKD liabilities — the exact reverse of the mechanism that destroyed 2024's distribution.

**Capital markets.** The Hong Kong market has returned **51.7% over the past year**, while HK REITs returned only **14% (R)**. Listed property has been a profound laggard in a raging equity bull market — a classic setup for either a violent catch-up or a permanent re-rating downward of the asset class.

### 4.2 Micro

- **Asset age.** Beijing Oriental Plaza opened 2000–01. At 25 years old, it competes against assets a decade or two newer in the CBD, Lize and Wangjing.
- **Location quality.** Genuinely first-tier: 1 East Chang'an Avenue, directly on Beijing's principal ceremonial axis, above the Wangfujing metro interchange, one block from Tiananmen Square and the Forbidden City. This is not replicable.
- **Single-asset concentration.** c.80% of NPI from one complex **(E)**.
- **External management.** No employees; a related-party Manager taking fees partly in units, which dilutes existing holders when the unit price is depressed.
- **Governance and disclosure.** Segment and tenant-level disclosure is thinner than at peer REITs, and the FX risk-management policy — specifically, running an unhedged cross-currency mismatch for over a decade — is the weakest single element of the story.

## 5. Product strength, brand and competitive position

### 5.1 How the products compare

**Beijing Oriental Plaza's office towers** compete on address, floorplate and metro connectivity rather than on modern specification. They are Grade A but not "new Grade A": tenants seeking LEED Platinum, high-efficiency floorplates and modern ESG credentials increasingly find newer product in the CBD core, Lize and Wangjing at materially lower rents. In a market where headline rent has fallen 16% in a year and landlords compete on rent-free periods **(R)**, an older trophy asset's brand premium erodes fastest — because the *relative* value of a prestigious address falls when the price gap to a newer, cheaper building widens.

**The Malls** occupy an awkward strategic position. The address is superb and footfall is high (tourist, office-worker, and transit-driven), but the tenant proposition sits below the true luxury tier now dominated by SKP Beijing and Taikoo Li, and above the value tier. In a bifurcating market, the middle is exactly where you do not want to be — and FY2025's RMB110m NPI decline **(R)** is the evidence.

**The hotels** are the strongest relative product. Grand Hyatt Beijing has been an anchor of the complex for over 25 years **(R)**, and Hyatt's Greater China portfolio is currently outperforming the national market **(R)**. Brand strength here is *rented*, not owned — it belongs to Hyatt and Marriott, and Hui Xian pays for it.

**Chongqing Metropolitan Oriental Plaza** is a good asset in a difficult market. Jiefangbei is Chongqing's premier CBD, but Chongqing has among the highest office vacancy rates of any major Chinese city.

### 5.2 Branding and marketing

The **"Oriental Plaza"** name has real equity in Beijing — it is a landmark, a metro destination, and a tourist waypoint. But brand equity in real estate is only monetisable through rent, and rent is set by the market clearing price of comparable space. The evidence of the last three years is that the brand has *not* protected pricing.

Critically, the **"Hui Xian REIT"** brand — as distinct from the asset brand — has been badly damaged. A unit price down c.90% from IPO, a DPU down 95% in four years, and a sub-1% yield mean the vehicle has effectively lost access to the equity capital markets. It cannot issue units accretively, which forecloses the standard REIT growth model of acquiring assets funded by new equity.

### 5.3 Economic moats: assessment

The honest answer is that **Hui Xian REIT has one genuine moat (irreplaceable hard assets) which is time-limited, and a set of pseudo-moats that do not survive scrutiny.**

## 8. The structural issue every investor must understand

Three features of the structure explain almost the entire investment case, and none is a cyclical problem.

### 8.1 The wasting leasehold

Beijing Oriental Plaza's commercial land use right runs to **6 December 2049 (R)** — approximately **23 years** from today. PRC law provides for automatic renewal of *residential* land use rights; commercial renewal is discretionary and the compensation/premium regime is unsettled. Cushman & Wakefield and other advisers have flagged this as an unresolved national policy question.

The practical implication is severe: **a book NAV built on a market valuation implicitly assumes renewal or terminal value.** If one instead treats the asset as a 23-year annuity, the maths changes completely. Discounting c.RMB1.1bn of NPI (flat, no growth) for 23 years at a 9% discount rate gives a present value of roughly **RMB10.5bn (E)** — versus an estimated book equity of c.RMB21–22bn **(E)**. On that basis, the "85% discount to NAV" is closer to **65–70%** — still large, but far less absurd than the headline. **The market is not being irrational about the discount; it is pricing a depreciating leasehold.**

### 8.2 The currency mismatch

At 31 December 2024, gross debt was **RMB5,777m, of which 66% was HKD-denominated and 34% RMB (R)**, against a 100% RMB asset and income base. **No currency hedging was in place at the balance-sheet date (R).**

Because the HKD is pegged to the USD, this is effectively an unhedged short-RMB/long-USD position of roughly **RMB3.8bn** sitting inside an RMB income vehicle. The consequences are documented:

| Event | Date | Amount repaid | Realised FX loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| Term loan partial prepayment | May 2024 | HK$1,400m | **(n.v.)** |
| Loan repayment | November 2024 | HK$1,800m | c.**RMB209m (R)** |
| FY2024 aggregate realised exchange losses | FY2024 | — | c.**RMB368m (R)** |
| Bank loan repayment (announced) | April 2026 | HK$800m | c.**RMB44m (R)** |

Chinese financial commentary counts **five debt repayments over two years, every one of them loss-making (R)**. The April 2026 loss of RMB44m alone equalled **157% of the entire FY2025 distributable amount and 438% of 1H2025's (R)**.

**This is not bad luck. It is an unhedged position held deliberately for over a decade.** A plain-vanilla cross-currency swap or a shift of the debt book into RMB would have eliminated it. The Manager's failure to do so is the single most important governance finding in this note.

**The one piece of good news:** the mechanism is symmetric. With USD/CNY at **6.72 and the RMB up 3.4% YTD (R)**, remaining HKD debt is now translating *favourably*. The same unhedged book that destroyed 2024's distribution should support 2026–27's — if the RMB holds.

### 8.3 The NPI-to-distribution gap

This is the reconciliation that no outside analyst can currently perform, and it should be the first question to management.

| Line | FY2025 | Tag |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | RMB2,209m | (R) |
| Net property income | c.RMB1,130m | (E) |
| **Total Amount Available for Distribution** | **RMB28m** | **(R)** |
| **Implied leakage** | **c.RMB1,100m (97% of NPI)** | **(D)** |

Plausible components, in order of likely size:

1. **Debt principal repayment** absorbed into the distribution calculation (the Trust repaid HK$1.4bn, HK$1.8bn and HK$800m across 2024–26 **(R)**) — deleveraging funded from distributable cash.
2. **Realised FX losses** on those repayments **(R)**.
3. **Finance costs** — RMB399m in 2023 **(R)**, likely c.RMB250–300m in 2025 as HIBOR fell **(E)**.
4. **PRC corporate income tax at 25%** plus **10% withholding** on outbound distributions.
5. **Hotel depreciation and capex reserve** — the hotels are PP&E, not investment property.
6. **Manager's fees**, partly settled in new units.

Even generously, items 3–6 total perhaps RMB600–700m **(E)**, leaving c.RMB400m unexplained without items 1 and 2. **The conclusion is that management is using unitholders' distributable income to repay debt, and crystallising currency losses while doing so.** That is a legitimate but undisclosed capital-allocation choice, and it is being made by a Manager 70%-owned by the largest unitholder.

### 13.4 Covenants

**Not publicly disclosed in accessible sources (n.v.).** Typical HK REIT unsecured term-loan covenants would include:

- maximum gearing / loan-to-value (the regulatory cap of 45–50% is the binding external constraint);
- minimum interest cover (commonly 2.0–2.5x);
- minimum consolidated net worth;
- change-of-control provisions referencing the sponsor's stake and the Manager's identity.

**Risk assessment: covenant risk is LOW.** At c.18–20% gearing **(E)** against a 45–50% cap, the Trust has enormous headroom. Even a further 40% write-down of the property portfolio would leave gearing around 30%. **This is not a distress story — it is a cash-distribution story.**

## 15. M&A: industry context, takeover candidacy and transaction history

### 15.1 Industry M&A context

The Chinese commercial real estate M&A market since 2021 has been characterised by:

- **Forced deleveraging** by mainland developers (Evergrande, Sunac, Country Garden), releasing prime commercial assets at distressed prices;
- **Cross-border capital retreat** — several major foreign investors have reduced China exposure, thinning the buyer pool and widening bid-ask spreads;
- **Domestic institutional inflow** via the fast-growing onshore **C-REIT** market, which offers PRC insurers and pension funds a compliant, RMB-native vehicle;
- **Consolidation of REIT managers** — the ARA to ESR (2022) and ESR privatisation (2024) sequence is directly relevant, since ARA owns 30% of Hui Xian's Manager **(R)**;
- **Take-privates of sub-scale listed vehicles** trading at deep NAV discounts, across both Hong Kong and Singapore.

### 15.2 Is Hui Xian REIT a takeover target?

**Our view: it is one of the more logical privatisation candidates in Asian listed real estate — and the fact that it has not happened is itself informative.**

**Arguments FOR (pros):**

| # | Argument |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Extreme NAV discount.** c.0.15x est. NAV **(E)** — a buyer could offer a 100% premium and still acquire at c.0.30x book. |
| 2 | **Concentrated, motivated ownership.** CK Asset holds c.35% **(R)** and controls 70% of the Manager **(R)**. It needs to acquire only c.55–60% of units to reach 90% and compulsorily acquire. |
| 3 | **Tiny absolute cost.** At a 100% premium (RMB1.00/unit), the c.65% not held by CK Asset costs roughly **RMB4.2bn (c.US$625m)** — trivially financeable for CK Asset. |
| 4 | **The listing serves no purpose.** The vehicle cannot raise equity accretively, has no analyst coverage, minimal liquidity, and a sub-1% yield. It bears listing costs for zero capital-markets benefit. |
| 5 | **Value would be released privately.** A private owner could hedge the currency book, gear to 40%, and extract cash without a distribution obligation or public disclosure of the leakage. |
| 6 | **Structural simplification.** The Sino-foreign JV and BVI chain are easier to restructure privately. |
| 7 | **Precedent.** ESR itself was taken private in 2024 at a substantial premium; sub-scale Asian REITs trading at deep discounts have been consistent take-private targets. |

**Arguments AGAINST (cons):**

| # | Argument |
|---|---|
| 1 | **The discount may be correct.** A 23-year leasehold with uncertain 2049 renewal is genuinely worth far less than book. A buyer paying 0.30x book may still be overpaying. |
| 2 | **Beijing office fundamentals are still deteriorating** — rents forecast −6.6% in 2026 **(R)**. No buyer wants to catch this knife. |
| 3 | **CK Asset already controls the economics** via the 70%-owned Manager and 35% stake. It captures fees and influence without paying a premium — *why buy the cow?* |
| 4 | **PRC regulatory complexity** — transferring interests in a Sino-foreign co-operative JV holding politically sensitive Chang'an Avenue land requires approvals that are not assured. |
| 5 | **Capital controls** constrain moving proceeds and add execution risk. |
| 6 | **SFC/REIT Code process** — a privatisation requires 75% approval of independent unitholders with no more than 10% against, a high bar at a depressed price. |
| 7 | **Reputational cost** for the sponsor of buying out minorities at 0.15–0.30x book after a 90% price decline since an IPO it sponsored. |

**Conclusion:** the *financial* logic for a take-private is overwhelming; the *political and reputational* logic is what has prevented it. Investors should treat privatisation as a **genuine but unpredictable option, not a base case** — and should note that CK Asset's rational strategy may be to let the price drift lower before acting.

**Implication for investors: do not underwrite an activist catalyst. There is no realistic mechanism by which minorities can force change at this vehicle.** The only agent capable of unlocking value is CK Asset itself, acting in its own interest.

## 22. Adversarial deep-dive: bull case, bear case, pre-mortem and contrarian view

### 22.1 The bull case

**1. Competitive advantage and moat sustainability.**
Beijing Oriental Plaza is genuinely irreplaceable. c.800,000 sqm on Chang'an Avenue at Wangfujing, integrated with the metro, a block from Tiananmen Square. No comparable site will ever be released again. In a market where new supply has stopped — **no new Beijing Grade A supply for twelve months, driving six consecutive quarters of falling vacancy (R)** — scarcity value in the prime core should reassert.

**2. The two structural drags are reversing simultaneously and mechanically.**
This is the strongest bull argument and it is arithmetic, not narrative:

- **Rates:** 1M HIBOR from **5.27% (Dec-23) to 2.63% (Aug-26) (R)**. On c.RMB5bn of debt, a c.264bp reduction is worth c.**RMB110–130m/year (E)**.
- **Currency:** USD/CNY at **6.72, RMB +3.4% YTD (R)**. The unhedged HKD book that destroyed RMB368m in FY2024 **(R)** now translates favourably.
- Against a distributable base of **RMB28m (R)**, these two swings alone could take distributable income to **RMB150–250m (E)** — a 5–9x increase — with *zero* operational improvement.

**3. Growth levers and potential positive surprises.**

- **Secular:** inbound travel to Beijing recovering; luxury hotel tier outperforming (Hyatt Greater China RevPAR **+7.2% in Q2-26 (R)**); Beijing office vacancy falling for six straight quarters **(R)**.
- **Self-help available and unused:** hedging the currency book (immediate, cheap); selling Shenyang and Chengdu hotels (c.RMB1.5–2.5bn **(E)**, retiring all HKD debt); executing the existing buyback mandate at 0.15x NAV.
- **Earnings surprise:** because there is **no analyst coverage (n.v.)**, there is no consensus to beat — but equally, any distribution normalisation would arrive entirely unanticipated by the market.

**4. Capital allocation — the bull reading.**
Deleveraging from RMB9.9bn (2018) to c.RMB5.0bn **(R)/(E)** has produced a fortress balance sheet: gearing c.18–20% against a 45–50% cap **(E)/(R)**, interest cover c.4x **(E)**, unsecured facilities, no refinancing distress in the worst Chinese property downturn on record. **The heavy lifting is done. From here, the cash that was going to lenders can go to unitholders.**

**5. Valuation.**
c.0.15x estimated NAV **(E)**, c.6x EV/NPI **(E)**, an implied c.16.6% cap rate on enterprise value **(E)** for assets that trade privately in Beijing at 4.5–6.5% **(E)**. Even applying a 50% haircut to book for the leasehold, the units trade at c.0.30x adjusted NAV.

### 22.2 The bear case — three risks that could permanently impair the business

**Risk 1: The leasehold is a terminal asset, and 2049 is closer than the market's mental model.**
The Beijing Oriental Plaza commercial land use right expires **6 December 2049 (R)** — 23 years away. PRC law provides automatic renewal only for residential land; commercial renewal is discretionary, and the premium payable is unlegislated. Valuers currently assume renewal. **If a future valuation cycle stops assuming that, the NAV halves overnight.** Worse, the impairment is *continuous and unavoidable*: every year that passes removes one year of a finite annuity. A 23-year DCF of RMB1.1bn of flat NPI at 9% gives c.RMB10.5bn of asset value **(E)** versus c.RMB27–28bn of carrying value **(E)**. **The write-downs of the last three years may be the beginning, not the end.**

**Risk 2: The NPI-to-distribution transmission is structurally broken and may never be repaired.**
FY2025: NPI c.RMB1,130m **(E)**, distributable RMB28m **(R)**. A **97.5% leakage rate**. Even if HIBOR falls to zero and the RMB appreciates 20%, the leakage from PRC tax, JV cash-extraction mechanics, hotel capex, Manager's fees and — critically — **management's revealed preference for debt repayment over distribution** does not automatically reverse. **A REIT that does not distribute is not a REIT; it is a closed-end fund with a fee drag, and closed-end funds trade at persistent discounts by construction.** There is no mechanism by which minorities can compel a change (Section 18.2).

**Risk 3: Beijing office and mid-market retail face structural, not cyclical, decline.**
Beijing Grade A rents are **−16.3% YoY with a further −6.6% forecast for 2026 (R)**. The complex is 25 years old and competes against newer, cheaper stock in the CBD, Lize and Wangjing. Retail NPI fell **28.6% in FY2025 (R)** — far worse than the market — while trophy competitors compounded (Taikoo Li Sanlitun sales **+63.2% (R)**). Hybrid working, a shrinking expatriate population, corporate cost discipline and the migration of luxury retail to SKP and Taikoo Li are all secular. **The office and retail portfolios may never see 2019 NPI again.**

**Additional bear points:** negative operating leverage (NPI falling 2.4x faster than revenue); c.30% of revenue from low-margin hotels in a market where national RevPAR fell 6% **(R)**; dilution via Manager's fee units and scrip; no analyst coverage and c.US$280m float; and an unhedged FX book that could resume producing losses if the RMB reverses.

### 22.3 Pre-mortem: it is 2029 and the units are at RMB0.15. What happened?

Working backwards from the failure:

1. **2026–27: the rate and FX windfall is captured by lenders, not unitholders.** HIBOR falls, the RMB strengthens, distributable income rises to c.RMB200m — and the Manager announces a further HK$1.5bn debt repayment. DPU rises to RMB0.01. The market concludes, correctly, that operating improvements will never reach unitholders. The yield stays under 2%.
2. **2027: the reversion cliff arrives.** Leases signed at 2021–22 peak rents roll onto 2027 market rents 30–35% lower. Office NPI falls from RMB685m to c.RMB500m. Occupancy holds — because rent was sacrificed to hold it.
3. **2027–28: retail deteriorates further.** The mall, already down 28.6% in one year, loses an anchor. A major asset-enhancement programme is announced requiring RMB800m of capex over three years. Distributions are suspended entirely "to fund the repositioning".
4. **2028: the valuers change their leasehold assumption.** With 21 years to expiry and no PRC policy clarity on commercial renewal, the independent valuer moves to a finite-life DCF. Investment property is written down 40%. Book NAV falls from RMB3.30 to c.RMB1.90. Gearing jumps to c.32% — still compliant, but the "85% discount" narrative that supported the units evaporates.
5. **2028–29: the sponsor acts, but not for minorities.** CK Asset, having watched the price fall to RMB0.20, makes a privatisation offer at RMB0.28 — a 40% premium to a collapsed price, and c.0.15x the reduced NAV. Minorities, exhausted and illiquid, accept.

**Probability we assign to a path materially like this: c.30%.** The pre-mortem's most important insight is that **steps 1 and 2 are already visibly in progress.**

### 22.4 Are current multiples too high?

**On income multiples: emphatically yes.** A 0.86% distribution yield against a peer group at **7.8–10.4% (R)** means investors are paying c.116x current distributions. On any income basis the units are among the most expensive REITs in the world.

**On asset multiples: no — and this is the crux.** c.0.15x book **(E)**, c.6x EV/NPI **(E)**, a c.16.6% implied cap rate **(E)**.

**Reconciling the two:** the correct question is not "which multiple is right" but **"what is the probability-weighted rate at which asset economics convert into unitholder cash?"** At the current 2.5% conversion rate, the units are expensive. At a normalised 25–30% conversion rate (achievable through hedging plus rate relief plus ceasing accelerated repayment), they are cheap. **The entire investment case is a bet on the conversion rate, and that rate is controlled by a Manager owned by the largest unitholder.**

### 22.5 The contrarian view: what the market refuses to see

**Contrarian thesis: the market is treating a reversible capital-structure problem as if it were an irreversible asset problem — and is therefore wrong about the direction of the next two years, while being right about the destination.**

Five specific things the market appears to be missing:

**1. The distribution collapse was almost entirely a *treasury* event, not an *operating* event.**
In 2024, revenue fell **0.3% (R)** while distributable income fell **89% (R)**. The cause was **RMB368m of realised FX losses (R)** on unhedged HKD debt. The market has priced the distribution collapse as evidence of asset failure. It was evidence of treasury failure — and treasury failures are fixable in an afternoon with a swap.

**2. The two variables that caused the collapse have already reversed, and the market has not marked to that.**
HIBOR **5.27% to 2.63% (R)**. USD/CNY weakening to **6.72, RMB +3.4% YTD (R)**. These are observable, published facts as of August 2026, not forecasts. The mechanical benefit to distributable income is c.**RMB110–130m from rates alone (E)** against a base of RMB28m. **The market is pricing 87001 off trailing FY2024–25 distributions that were depressed by conditions which no longer exist.**

**3. The deleveraging is nearly complete, which changes the cash-flow claim.**
Debt has fallen from RMB9.9bn (2018) to c.RMB5.0bn **(R)/(E)**, gearing to c.18–20% against a 45–50% cap **(E)/(R)**. There is a finite amount of debt left to repay. Once it is repaid, the cash has nowhere to go *but* distributions — unless the Manager finds a new use for it, which is precisely the risk.

**4. The disposal option is large, obvious and completely unpriced.**
Shenyang and Chengdu are c.8% of NPI **(E)** and probably 15–20% of gross asset value. Selling them could raise **RMB1.5–2.5bn (E)** — enough to extinguish the entire HKD debt book, eliminate the FX mismatch permanently, remove hotel operating volatility, and reset distributable income structurally higher. **This option costs nothing to hold and the market ascribes it zero value.**

**5. Everyone is anchored on the 85% NAV discount, which is the wrong number in both directions.**
Bulls quote 0.15x book and call it a layup; bears note the assets are impaired and call it a value trap. **Both are using the wrong denominator.** The right denominator is a 23-year finite-life DCF, which gives c.RMB10.5bn of asset value **(E)**, c.RMB6.5bn of equity value after net debt, and c.**RMB1.00 per unit**. Against RMB0.50, that is a 100% upside — attractive, but a *normal* deep-value opportunity, not a six-bagger. **The market is not irrational; it is just using an inflated book value as its reference point, which makes both the bull and bear arguments sloppier than they should be.**

**The uncomfortable conclusion:** the contrarian is right that the next two years should surprise positively, and the consensus is right that the destination is impairment. **This is a trade, not an investment.** The correct posture is to own it for the FX-and-rates normalisation into 2027, with a hard exit discipline, and never to confuse it with a long-duration income holding.

## 25. The short-seller's case: dismantling the bull argument

*What follows is written deliberately in the voice of a sceptical short-seller, as a stress test of the long thesis. It is an adversarial exercise, not our house view.*

### 25.1 What structurally breaks the way this company makes money

**It is already broken. The market simply has not finished pricing it.**

This is not a REIT. A REIT converts property income into unitholder distributions. This vehicle converts **RMB1.13bn of net property income into RMB28m of distributions — a 2.5% conversion rate.** The other 97.5% goes to Chinese tax authorities, Hong Kong banks, a related-party manager, and the foreign exchange market. **You are not buying a rent roll. You are buying the residual after four parties with superior claims have taken theirs.**

And the residual is geared. Because c.RMB765m of costs below NPI are fixed **(E)**, a 20% fall in NPI cuts the distribution by 79% **(D)**. NPI has just fallen 13.3% in one year **(E)** and revenue has fallen for five consecutive half-years **(R)**. **Do that maths forward and the distribution goes to zero before the revenue decline reaches 15%.**

### 25.2 Where is revenue concentrated, and what happens if it shifts?

**c.80% of NPI from a single building (E).** Not a single market, not a single city — a single *building*, 25 years old, on a leasehold expiring in 2049 **(R)**.

Within it, the concentration compounds: office is c.60% of NPI **(E)** in a market where rents fell **16.3% YoY (R)** with another **6.6% forecast (R)**. If Beijing office rents fall a further 25% cumulatively by 2029 — entirely plausible given hybrid working, a shrinking expatriate base, and the Lize/Wangjing supply wave — office NPI goes from RMB685m to c.RMB510m. **That alone eliminates the distribution.**

Tenant concentration is **undisclosed (n.v.)**. In a 25-year-old Beijing tower whose natural tenant base is MNCs and SOEs, the probability that a handful of names dominate is high. **Management has been asked and has not told you.**

### 25.3 Why the moat is weaker than bulls think

Bulls say "irreplaceable location". Three problems:

1. **Irreplaceable does not mean profitable.** Scarcity sets a *ceiling* on value, not a *floor* on rent. Rent is set by the marginal competing square metre, and there are millions of them in Beijing, newer and cheaper. Office NPI is down **24% from its disclosed peak (R)/(D)**.
2. **You do not own it in perpetuity — you rent it from the PRC state until 2049 (R).** Twenty-three years. Every valuation on the balance sheet assumes renewal on unlegislated terms from a government under fiscal pressure with total discretion. **The moat has an expiry date printed on it.**
3. **The brand belongs to somebody else.** Grand Hyatt, Westin, Sheraton — Hui Xian *pays* for those brands. On c.30% of revenue, the moat is licensed and terminable.

### 25.4 The most dangerous competitor bulls are underestimating

**Not Swire, not SKP. The onshore C-REIT market.**

Chinese insurers and pension funds now buy prime Chinese commercial property through onshore RMB REITs: no offshore holding chain, no Sino-foreign JV, no 10% withholding on outbound dividends, no HKD funding mismatch, no capital-control friction. **They own the same asset class with none of Hui Xian's structural leakage — which means they can pay more for assets and still distribute more to investors.**

That does two things: it compresses the cap rate at which Hui Xian could ever sell (good, once) and it permanently removes Hui Xian's reason to exist as a listed vehicle (bad, forever). **Hui Xian's structure was designed in 2011 to solve a problem — offshore access to RMB property income — that China has since solved domestically and better.** The vehicle is a legacy workaround for a closed capital account that is no longer as closed.

**Second dangerous competitor: the sponsor.** CK Asset holds 35% of units and 70% of the Manager **(R)**. It collects fees whatever happens and has no obligation to bid for your units. Every year the price falls, its eventual take-private gets cheaper. **Time is on its side and against yours.**

### 25.5 The worst capital allocation decisions

| Decision | Cost |
|---|---|
| **Fifteen years of zero FX hedging** | c.**RMB368m realised in FY2024 alone (R)**, plus c.RMB209m (Nov-24) and RMB44m (Apr-26) crystallisations **(R)**. Cumulatively **more than 20 years of distributions at the current DPU** |
| **Chongqing acquisition, March 2015** | Bought near the peak into what became one of China's weakest office markets. c.RMB3–4bn deployed **(E)** for c.12% of NPI **(E)** with no growth in eleven years |
| **Hotel acquisitions from the sponsor, 2011–12** | A related-party purchase that permanently diluted NPI margin by adding c.30% of low-margin, capital-intensive operating revenue |
| **Repaying HK$4.0bn of debt from distributable cash, 2024–26** | Transferred c.RMB4bn of unitholder cash flow to lenders while crystallising c.RMB250m-plus of FX losses doing it **(R)** |
| **Never exercising the buyback mandate at 0.15x NAV** | Foregone accretion of c.RMB2.80 of NAV per RMB0.50 deployed **(E)** |
| **Issuing units to the Manager at 0.15x NAV** | Direct value transfer from unitholders to a related party **(R)** |

**Eleven years without a single acquisition or disposal is not conservatism. It is the absence of a strategy — and it is exactly what you would expect from a manager paid on assets rather than on distributions.**

### 25.6 Related-party transactions, accounting and incentives

- **The Manager is 70%-owned by the 35% unitholder (R).** Every capital-allocation decision — repay debt, distribute, buy back, sell assets — is made by an agent of the largest holder.
- **The Manager takes fees in units (R)**, diluting minorities at an 85% discount while the buyback mandate sits unused.
- **The hotels were bought from the sponsor (R).**
- **Connected-party transaction waivers were granted by the SFC at authorisation and "from time to time thereafter" (R)** — an ongoing regime of exemptions from the disclosure and unitholder-approval requirements of Chapter 8 of the REIT Code. **Investors should read the current waiver terms in full before assuming standard protections apply.**
- **The Manager's fee quantum is not disclosed in accessible sources (n.v.).**

### 25.7 What must be true to justify RMB0.50?

At RMB0.50 and 6.52bn units, the market capitalisation is c.RMB3.26bn, and enterprise value c.RMB6.8bn **(E)**. To justify that, **all** of the following must hold:

1. Beijing office NPI stabilises within two years after a 24% peak-to-trough decline;
2. Retail NPI stops falling after a 28.6% single-year collapse;
3. The RMB does not weaken back through 7.20;
4. HIBOR stays at or below 2.6%;
5. Management stops using distributable cash to repay debt;
6. The 2049 leasehold continues to be valued on a going-concern (renewal-assumed) basis;
7. No major capex event at a 25-year-old complex or four ageing hotels;
8. No further dilution from Manager's fee units.

**Eight conditions, each individually uncertain. Assign 70% probability to each independently and the joint probability is 6%.** They are of course correlated — but the point stands: **the current price is not a margin of safety, it is a compound bet.**

### 25.8 If growth disappoints by 20–30%

Already quantified in Section 23.4 and it is brutal: **a 20% NPI shortfall cuts distributable income by 79%; a 30% shortfall eliminates it.** At a 30% shortfall the units have no income value at all and are worth only whatever a controlling buyer will pay for a shrinking leasehold — probably **RMB0.15–0.25**.

### 25.9 The single scenario that permanently impairs the business

**Beijing announces the commercial land use right renewal framework, and it requires payment of a substantial market-value premium at expiry.**

That is the killer. It is a *policy* event, not a market event, so no operational improvement offsets it. The moment it lands, every independent valuer in China must move commercial leaseholds to finite-life DCFs. Hui Xian's investment property — carried at an estimated RMB27–28bn **(E)** — reprices toward a 23-year annuity worth c.RMB10.5bn **(E)**. Book NAV falls from c.RMB3.30 to c.RMB1.05 **(E)**. Gearing roughly doubles. The "85% discount" that anchors every bull thesis simply ceases to exist.

**Plausibility: moderate and rising.** China's land finance model is under severe fiscal strain; local governments need revenue; the first tranche of major commercial land use rights granted in the 1990s begins expiring in the 2040s, and the policy vacuum is increasingly untenable. **We would put 20–30% on a clarifying policy event before 2030, and the direction of any such clarification is more likely to favour the state than the leaseholder.**

## 27. Conclusion

Hui Xian REIT is a case study in how a world-class asset can be rendered nearly worthless to its owners by the structure wrapped around it.

The building is extraordinary. The location cannot be reproduced. The balance sheet is genuinely conservative — c.18–20% gearing against a 45–50% cap, no covenant stress through the worst Chinese property downturn on record. On asset multiples the units are among the cheapest listed real estate anywhere on earth.

And yet: **RMB1.13bn of net property income produced RMB28m of distributions in 2025.** Unitholders received 2.5 fen of every yuan the properties earned. The rest went to the tax authorities, the banks, the currency market and a manager owned by the largest unitholder. A 0.86% yield against peers at 7–10% is not a mispricing; it is the market's accurate assessment of a broken transmission mechanism.

The genuinely interesting question — and the reason this is a HOLD rather than a SELL — is that **the two forces that broke the transmission are reversing right now, mechanically and observably.** HIBOR has fallen 264bp from its peak. The RMB has appreciated 3.4% this year. Neither requires a single tenant to sign a single lease. Together they are worth several multiples of the current distribution, and the market is still pricing off trailing distributions that were depressed by conditions which no longer exist.

But the same Manager that ran an unhedged currency book for fifteen years, that has never bought back a unit at 0.15x book while issuing units to itself, and that has repaid HK$4.0bn of debt out of unitholders' distributable cash while crystallising RMB250m of avoidable losses, will decide where that windfall goes. On the evidence, it goes to the lenders.

And behind all of it sits a clock: **6 December 2049.** Twenty-three years. Every valuation on the balance sheet assumes a renewal that no law guarantees.

**Own this for the 2026–27 normalisation if you must, in small size, with a hard exit. Do not own it for the income, and do not own it for 2049.**
