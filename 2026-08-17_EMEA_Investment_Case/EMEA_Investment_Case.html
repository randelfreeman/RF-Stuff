Europe's Stagflation Rally

The Stoxx 600 is at a record high. The eurozone economy shrank last quarter and the ECB is raising rates. Both of those facts are true, and the reason they can be true at once is the entire investment case.

The call
Moderate overweight EMEA, funded from US large-cap — but own the war-resilient earnings, not the index.

The cheap-Europe argument you have heard is roughly half right and badly reasoned. The valuation gap is real. The macro is genuinely bad. The resolution is that the earnings driving this market are produced by sectors the bad macro is helping. Buy those. Do not buy a European economic recovery — that is a separate trade, it has not started, and it needs a war to end first.

The setup

A record that nobody is celebrating

The pan-European Stoxx 600 closed at 656.86 on 4 August, an all-time high, and is up roughly 10% year-to-date. The German DAX, French CAC 40 and Italian FTSE MIB have all printed record peaks. The FTSE 100 entered the year through 10,000 for the first time.

Europe is still behind North America in 2026 — 10% against 13.5%. But Goldman Sachs makes the point that gets lost: measured from the start of 2025, through both a tariff shock and an energy crisis, the Stoxx has outperformed the S&P 500. European banks have beaten the Magnificent 7 since 2022. The "Europe is uninvestable" narrative is not a view, it is an unchecked habit.

The contradiction

Records printed into a contracting economy

Here is what most notes on European equities skip, because it is inconvenient.

The proximate cause is the US–Israel war on Iran, which drove Brent to a peak above $126 on 30 April. Europe is a large net energy importer, so the shock landed directly on both inflation and real incomes. In June the ECB lifted the deposit rate from 2.00% to 2.25% — its first increase in nearly three years — abandoning the easing path the market had assumed all year.

A market at all-time highs, an economy in contraction, and a central bank tightening into it. If that combination does not make you uncomfortable, you have not understood the position.

The resolution

The index is not the economy

The Stoxx 600 is a claim on global earnings that happen to be listed in Europe — roughly 60% of its revenue is earned outside the region. Its largest sectors are financials, pharma, technology, energy, utilities, telecoms and aerospace. Almost none of that is a bet on eurozone GDP.

And the three engines of the 2026 rally are each helped by the same macro that is hurting the domestic economy:

Banks, the largest sector, are up 22% year-to-date. The ECB hike is a direct tailwind — it defers the net-interest-income compression the market had priced in for 2026. Consolidation and strong margins are doing the rest. A hawkish ECB is bullish for the biggest thing you own. Energy repriced upward on the war and led one of Europe's strongest quarters of earnings growth in years. Semiconductors are riding global AI capex, not European demand.

Meanwhile the parts genuinely exposed to a weak European consumer have already been marked down hard.

That dispersion is the most important chart in this note. A market that pays 371% for a wafer maker and takes 52% off a mass-market carmaker is discriminating, not euphoric. It changes what "buying Europe" should mean — index exposure buys you the average of two violently opposed trades.

Why now

Four supports under the position

1  Earnings are actually delivering

Q2 growth came in around 17%, the strongest in four years, led by energy and technology. Revision breadth is running 65% upward against 35% downward and has been improving since late May. Consensus for Q3 and full-year 2026 has moved to roughly 20% EPS growth, up from mid-teens three months ago. This is a market being repriced on rising estimates, not just a rising multiple.

The honest caveat: the sell-side spread here is enormous. Goldman models 5% EPS growth for 2026; UBS models 14%; bottom-up consensus says 20%. That is not a forecast, it is a range of opinion four times wide. Underwrite the low end and treat anything above it as upside.

2  The valuation gap is still open

Part of that gap is justified — sector mix and genuinely lower structural growth explain a chunk of it. But not all of it, and the residual is wide enough that you are paid for holding the part that is not explained. One correction to the usual pitch: Europe's 2.4% trailing dividend yield versus the S&P's 1.06% overstates the income advantage, because US buybacks lift American total shareholder yield to 2.5–3.0%. Europe is dividend-led, the US is buyback-led; the real yield gap is narrow.

3  Positioning is mid-cycle, not crowded

This matters because it means the sentiment trade is not finished. A net 2% overweight is not a crowded position — it is the shallow end of a range that touched net 35% as recently as February.

4  The reallocation pool is structural

Between 2009 and early 2026, US equity funds attracted roughly €224bn of net inflows while European funds shed about €316bn — a divergence exceeding €540bn. That flow gap does not need to reverse for European equities to work. It only needs to stop getting worse.

Currency

The FX point that gets double-counted

EUR/USD sits at 1.1564. Goldman targets roughly 1.25 by year-end; J.P. Morgan and ING target 1.22. For a dollar-based investor that is a material unhedged tailwind on top of local returns.

But the same euro strength is a headwind to the earnings themselves. Goldman explicitly cut its European EPS forecast by two to three percentage points because of expected dollar weakness — an index earning 60% of revenue abroad translates a strong euro into weaker reported profit.

Anyone showing you a 20% EPS number and a 1.25 euro in the same bull case is counting the same move twice. They partly cancel.

Practical conclusion: stay unhedged as a USD investor — the currency gain is first-order and the translation drag second-order — but do not underwrite both legs at once. If the euro runs to 1.25, expect the earnings number to be revised toward the lower end of that four-times-wide sell-side range.

Implementation

What to own, in order of conviction

  1. 1
    European banksHighest conviction

    The largest index sector, up 22% year-to-date and still not expensive. The ECB hiking cycle is the tailwind, consolidation is live, and margins are holding. This is the one position that works because of the bad macro rather than despite it — which is exactly what you want when the macro is the main risk.

  2. 2
    Defence & infrastructureStructural

    German fiscal spending is the strongest single growth impulse in 2026, worth an estimated 0.5pp cumulatively to euro-area growth over 2025–28 on ECB numbers. It is legislated, multi-year, and largely insensitive to the business cycle. Ongoing conflict makes reversal politically impossible in the forecast horizon.

  3. 3
    EnergyInternal hedge

    Hold it specifically as the offset to the Iran risk carried by everything else in the book. If the ceasefire collapses and Brent runs back toward $126, this is the position that protects the rest. You are buying it for the correlation, not the multiple.

  4. 4
    Emerging EMEACheapest genuine opportunity

    Greece, Poland and Czechia have advanced 16–25% year-to-date and still trade at discounts to eurozone peers with real convergence room. South African financials are leading on GNU policy continuity and macro stability. Saudi is more balanced — valuations have reset and bank earnings are resilient, but lower oil and tight liquidity offset; the expected easing of foreign ownership limits in 2026 is a concrete catalyst worth pre-positioning for.

  5. 5
    Domestic cyclicalsOption, not a position

    Autos and luxury are down far enough that a durable ceasefire plus falling energy costs would re-rate them violently. Size this as premium you can afford to lose entirely, because the trigger is genuinely binary and outside your control.

Underweight: broad domestic consumer discretionary and rate-sensitive real estate. And do not chase the semiconductor leadership after moves of +101% to +371% into a group that is already showing a pullback — that is where the froth in this market actually sits, and it is the one part of Europe that carries US-style AI concentration risk.

Risk

What kills this trade

Ceasefire collapseDominant & binary

Washington and Tehran have "mostly agreed" a 60-day memorandum extending the ceasefire, but strikes continue — ballistic missiles at Kuwait, drones toward the Strait of Hormuz — and UBS notes little evidence of improving vessel traffic or energy flows. Brent has round-tripped from $126 on 30 April to about $71. A move back above $100 restores the inflation impulse, forces further ECB tightening and compresses the multiple.

Exit trigger: Brent sustained > $100

Core inflation above 3%

The market is priced for the June hike being close to one-and-done. A core print above 3% turns the bank thesis inside out — from an NII tailwind into credit costs and recession fear, which would take out the highest-conviction leg of the position.

Exit trigger: euro-area core CPI > 3.0%

Earnings breadth rolls over

Revision breadth at 65% up / 35% down is the cleanest single confirmation that the earnings leg is intact. Below 50% the thesis has lost its main support, regardless of what the valuation gap says.

Exit trigger: upward revisions < 50% of total

Consensus converges downward

If bottom-up 2026 EPS drifts from 20% toward Goldman's 5% rather than the reverse, the market is paying 15.9x for materially less growth than advertised — and the discount to the US stops looking like an opportunity.

Watch: FY26 consensus EPS revisions

AI capex disappointmentPartly cushioned

This hits the semiconductor leadership hardest, and those names are extended. Note the asymmetry, though: Europe's lower AI concentration is a relative cushion versus the US in that scenario — one of the few places where the region's structural weakness becomes a defensive advantage.

Own the first leg now. Keep the option on the second. The war is the hinge on both.
